Prisoner’s dilemma and modelling
Problems of this kind that happen in real life : competition between companies, conflicts, social behavior of various animals, world war, etc... [Axelrod, 1992]
This very simple model is an approach to understand and discuss  the problems of individualistic behavior and collective cooperation. It is therefore widely used in ecology, economy and social science.

Iterated prisoner ’s dilemma
The game is more interesting when the end of the game is not known.  The strategy is built on the perception of the behaviour of the opponent rather than a computation of the expected payoffs at the end of the interaction. The adaptation is the important concept.
If I know that my opponent always defect, I will defect.
If I know that my opponent always cooperate, I will defect.
The strategy Tit for Tat has been the winner of a tournament organised by Axelrod. First cooperate, then always imitate your opponent’s behaviour

Game theory and evolution
Strategy: behavioural phenotype
 p is a proportion of an animal population with alternative strategy J trying to invade (1-p) which has strategy I.
The payoff of the strategy I as opposed to  J is E(I,J).
 W is the fitness, the adaptive value (in the Darwinian sense).

ESS
ESS (Evolutionary Stable Strategy): when a strategy is dominant, it cannot be invaded by any rare mutant strategy
W0  is initial individual fitness. The fitness of each strategy is :
W(I) = W0 + (1-p)E(I,I) + pE(I,J)
W(J) = W0 + (1-p)E(J,I) + pE(J,J)
I is an ESS if one of the conditions applies:
a) E(I,I) > E(J,I)
b) E(I,I) = E(J,I)  and E(I,J) > E(J,J)

The Hawk-Dove game
Maynard Smith and Price (1973)
Animal mating behaviour
Two strategies
Hawk: escalate and continue until injured or until opponent retreats (H)
Dove : display; retreat at once if opponent escalates (D)

Mixed strategy
Strategy I: adopt H with probability P and adopt D with  1-P
If I is an ESS, then
E(H,I) = E(D,I)
PE(H,H) + (1- P)E(H,D) = PE(D,H) + (1- P)E(D,D)
0.5(V-C)P + V(1-P) = 0.5V(1-P)
P = V/C
